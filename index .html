About

A little about me

I’m a historian of West Africa, with a particular focus on Ghana. My research explores the search for economic sovereignty, mining and environmental change, and women’s roles in healing and medicine. I’m particularly interested in how African leaders and ordinary people have understood and contested the promises and costs of development, how mining has shaped relationships between people, the state, and the environment, and how women’s knowledge and healing practices have influenced social and cultural life. My work brings together African political economy, environmental history, and the histories of gender, religion, and medicine.

I combine archival research, oral history, and spatial analysis, reading government records, corporate archives, and visual sources alongside the memories of people who lived through these changes. My research has been supported by Princeton’s Department of History, the Princeton Institute for International and Regional Studies (PIIRS), and the Africa World Initiative (AWI).

Before Princeton, I earned my M.A. in History at Western Illinois University and my B.A. in History at the University of Education, Winneba, in Ghana. Along the way I’ve taught in secondary school and university classrooms, edited a student history journal, and helped bring scholarship to wider audiences through public exhibitions and academic publishing.

Research

The questions that drive my work

Dissertation

The Price of Sovereignty: Sacrifice, Development, and Violence in Ghana, 1951–1966

Why did Ghana’s first postcolonial government come to see deep social, environmental, and economic costs as sacrifices worth making for economic sovereignty, and how did citizens respond? Focusing on the Volta River Project, the cocoa industry, and the mining sector, I trace how displacement, ecological disruption, labor discipline, and economic hardship became part of the language of decolonization, and how Ghanaians answered with acceptance, negotiation, and counterclaims.

Drawing on government records, institutional and corporate archives, visual sources, and oral histories, the project follows political leaders, local intermediaries, and ordinary Ghanaians through the same story.

01

Sacrifice as politics

Brings sacrifice, usually treated as a religious or ritual category, into the political history of development, revealing the moral language of sovereignty and national obligation.

02

One developmental state

Places infrastructure, agriculture, and extractive industry within a single history of Ghana’s developmental state.

03

From above and below

Analyzes elites and ordinary citizens within the same field of political negotiation.

Next book project

Economic sovereignty across Ghana, Guinea, and Tanzania

Three states with different colonial inheritances, political ideologies, and models of development all converged on sacrifice as a language of economic independence. This project asks why, how religious and sociocultural ideas gave those demands different meanings in each place, and why citizens answered with varying forms of consent, negotiation, and resistance.

Teaching & mentoring

My teaching turns historical inquiry into skills students can practice beyond the classroom. In Ghana, I trained students in oral history, archival research, source criticism, and historical argument, then had them design research questions, conduct interviews, and present short research papers of their own. When transportation costs kept students from the archives, I brought my own archival materials into the classroom, and I led visits to Cape Coast and Elmina Castles to connect the histories of slavery and the Atlantic world to place and tangible evidence.

I see mentoring as sustained intellectual support that helps students build confidence, independence, and a scholarly voice. As a Writing Center tutor at Western Illinois University, I worked with students across the semester to read rubrics, sharpen arguments, and strengthen their writing; five became regular mentees. I’ve also mentored master’s and doctoral students on their writing, research questions, and sources.
